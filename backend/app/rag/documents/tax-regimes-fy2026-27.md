## The two regimes

India runs two parallel personal income tax systems. The **new regime** is the
default: if you do not actively opt out, you are taxed under it. The **old
regime** must be chosen deliberately, and it is the only one where most
deductions survive.

FY 2026-27 is the first year assessed under the **Income-tax Act, 2025**, which
replaced the Income-tax Act, 1961 with effect from 1 April 2026. The
substance of most provisions carried over; the section numbers did not. Returns
for FY 2025-26 were still filed under the 1961 Act.

## New regime slabs (FY 2026-27)

| Total income | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Up to ₹4,00,000 | Nil |
| ₹4,00,001 – ₹8,00,000 | 5% |
| ₹8,00,001 – ₹12,00,000 | 10% |
| ₹12,00,001 – ₹16,00,000 | 15% |
| ₹16,00,001 – ₹20,00,000 | 20% |
| ₹20,00,001 – ₹24,00,000 | 25% |
| Above ₹24,00,000 | 30% |

- Basic exemption: ₹4,00,000
- Standard deduction on salary: ₹75,000
- Section 87A rebate: up to ₹60,000, which makes taxable income up to
  ₹12,00,000 effectively tax-free. For a salaried person the standard deduction
  stacks on top, so gross salary up to roughly ₹12,75,000 can carry nil tax.

## Old regime slabs (FY 2026-27)

| Total income | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Up to ₹2,50,000 | Nil |
| ₹2,50,001 – ₹5,00,000 | 5% |
| ₹5,00,001 – ₹10,00,000 | 20% |
| Above ₹10,00,000 | 30% |

- Basic exemption: ₹2,50,000 (higher for senior and super-senior citizens)
- Standard deduction on salary: ₹50,000
- Section 87A rebate: ₹12,500, making taxable income up to ₹5,00,000 tax-free

## Surcharge and cess (both regimes)

Surcharge applies on the tax amount, not on income:

| Total income | Surcharge |
| --- | --- |
| Up to ₹50 lakh | Nil |
| ₹50 lakh – ₹1 crore | 10% |
| ₹1 crore – ₹2 crore | 15% |
| ₹2 crore – ₹5 crore | 25% |
| Above ₹5 crore | 25% (new regime), 37% (old regime) |

Health and education cess of **4%** is charged on tax plus surcharge in both
regimes.

## Which regime wins

The new regime has wider slabs and a much larger rebate. The old regime only
beats it when your deductions are large enough to close that gap. As a rough
rule, the old regime starts making sense once total deductions and exemptions
exceed roughly ₹4.5–5 lakh — which in practice means you are claiming a full
₹1.5 lakh under 80C, health insurance, NPS, and substantial HRA or home loan
interest together.

Someone with no home loan, modest rent, and partial 80C investments is almost
always better off on the new regime. Do the arithmetic both ways before
choosing; the difference is frequently ₹20,000–₹80,000 a year.

A salaried taxpayer without business income may switch regimes each year at the
time of filing. Those with business or professional income face restrictions on
switching back and forth.

## Section renumbering under the Income-tax Act, 2025

The familiar names still describe the benefit, but the statutory references
changed from FY 2026-27:

| Old (1961 Act) | New (2025 Act) |
| --- | --- |
| 80C | 123 |
| 80D | 126 |
| 80E | 129 |
| 80EE / 80EEA | 130 / 131 |
| 80G | 133 |
| 87A | 156 |
| 24(b) | 20 |
| Exemptions under section 10 | Schedule II |

Eligible 80C-type instruments are now listed in Schedule XV of the new Act.
Mappings for some provisions — notably 80CCD, 80TTA/80TTB, HRA and the capital
gains sections — should be checked against the CBDT concordance table on the
e-filing portal before being quoted in a filing. Limits and eligibility were
broadly preserved; only the numbering moved.

## Caveat

These figures are for FY 2026-27 and were current as of September 2026. Tax law
changes with each Finance Act. Verify against the income tax department's
official material before acting, and consult a chartered accountant for
anything material.
