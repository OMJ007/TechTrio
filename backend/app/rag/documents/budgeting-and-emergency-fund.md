## The 50/30/20 starting point

Divide take-home pay into 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings and investments.
Needs are rent, utilities, groceries, transport, insurance, minimum loan
payments. Wants are everything discretionary. The final fifth goes out before
it can be spent.

It is a diagnostic, not a law. In an expensive metro on an early-career salary,
needs alone can exceed 50% and the honest response is to target a lower savings
rate temporarily rather than pretend. In a low-cost city on a good salary, 20%
is far too modest a target.

The number that actually matters is the savings rate, and the direction it is
moving.

## The emergency fund

Three to six months of essential expenses — not income, expenses — held
somewhere you can reach within a day or two.

Six months if your income is variable, you are the sole earner, you work in a
volatile sector, or you support dependants. Three months if you have stable
salaried income, a working spouse, and few obligations.

Keep it in a savings account or a liquid mutual fund. Not equity, which can be
down 30% precisely when you lose your job, and not in anything with a lock-in.
The return on this money is irrelevant; its job is availability.

This comes before investing. An investor without a buffer is forced to sell
assets at the worst possible time, and that single event usually costs more
than years of optimising returns.

## Debt, in order of urgency

**Credit card revolving balances** at 36–48% annualised. Nothing else in
personal finance comes close to this rate. Clear these before investing in
anything — no investment reliably returns 40%, so repayment is the highest
guaranteed return available to you.

**Personal loans** at 12–24%. Next.

**Car loans** at 9–12%. Then.

**Home loans and education loans** at 8–9%, with tax benefits attached. These
are not urgent. Prepaying a home loan competes directly with investing, and at
current rates the comparison is close enough that either choice is defensible.

Never take a loan to invest.

## Debt-to-income

Total EMIs should stay below 40% of take-home pay, and below 30% is more
comfortable. Lenders use this ratio to decide what you can borrow; use it
yourself to decide what you should.

A ratio above 50% means any interruption in income — a job change, a medical
event — becomes a crisis rather than an inconvenience.

## Credit score

Above 750 gets you the best rates available; below 650 gets refused or repriced
sharply. Over a ₹50 lakh home loan, a 0.5% rate difference is several lakh
rupees across the tenure, so this is worth attending to well before you apply.

What moves it: paying every bill on time, without exception — payment history
dominates the score. Keeping credit utilisation below 30% of your limit. Not
closing old cards, since length of credit history counts. Not applying for
several cards or loans in a short window, since each hard enquiry costs points.

Check your report annually. Errors are common and take weeks to correct, which
is fine in advance and disastrous mid-application.

## Lifestyle inflation

The most reliable destroyer of a savings rate is a raise. Income rises, and
spending rises to meet it within two months, leaving the savings rate exactly
where it was.

The fix is mechanical: on every raise, direct a fixed share of the increase to
investments through an automatic transfer before adjusting anything else. You
never see it, so you never adapt to it.
