## Three different NPS deductions

Section 80CCD has three limbs and they behave very differently, which is where
most of the confusion comes from.

**80CCD(1) — your own contribution, inside the 80C limit**
Counts toward the same ₹1.5 lakh ceiling as 80C. No extra room. Old regime only.

**80CCD(1B) — your own contribution, an extra ₹50,000**
An additional ₹50,000 deduction **over and above** the ₹1.5 lakh limit, for
contributions to your NPS Tier-I account. At the 30% slab this is worth ₹15,600
including cess. **Old regime only — disallowed under the new regime.**

**80CCD(2) — your employer's contribution**
A deduction for what your *employer* puts into your NPS, capped at **14% of
salary** (basic plus dearness allowance). This one is **available under both
regimes**, which makes it the single most valuable deduction still standing for
anyone on the new regime.

## Why 80CCD(2) is the one to act on

If you are on the new regime — which most people now are by default — almost
every deduction you might have used is gone. 80CCD(2) is the conspicuous
exception.

The mechanism is a salary restructuring, not an investment decision. You ask
your employer to route a portion of your CTC into corporate NPS instead of
paying it as taxable salary. The money is still yours; it simply arrives in
your retirement account untaxed rather than in your bank account taxed.

On a ₹20 lakh salary with basic of ₹10 lakh, a 14% employer NPS contribution is
₹1.4 lakh of income that never enters the tax net. At the 30% slab that is
roughly ₹43,000 a year saved, on the new regime, where you were told no
deductions were available.

The catch: your employer must offer corporate NPS and must agree to the
restructuring. Many do; most employees never ask. It also locks the money until
60, which is the point of it but a real constraint if your emergency fund is
thin.

## What NPS actually is

A government-regulated retirement account. You choose an asset mix — equity
(capped at 75% under the active choice), corporate bonds, government
securities — and a pension fund manager, both changeable later. Charges are the
lowest of any managed product in India by a wide margin.

**Tier-I** is the retirement account with the tax benefits and the lock-in to
age 60. **Tier-II** is an open-access account with no lock-in and no tax
benefit for most subscribers — effectively a low-cost mutual fund.

## At maturity

At 60 you may withdraw up to **60% as a lump sum, entirely tax-free**. The
remaining 40% must buy an annuity, and the annuity income is taxable as regular
income in the years you receive it.

That annuity requirement is the main criticism of NPS: annuity rates in India
are unexciting, and you are compelled into one. Weigh that against the cost
advantage and the 80CCD(2) benefit rather than treating either side as settling
the argument.

## Where it fits

NPS is a retirement product first and a tax product second. It suits money you
genuinely will not need before 60. It does not replace an emergency fund, and
it should not be the destination for savings earmarked for a house or
education.

A common sequence: EPF as the base, then 80C filled with ELSS, then NPS for the
extra ₹50,000 under 80CCD(1B) if on the old regime — and for anyone on the new
regime, corporate NPS under 80CCD(2) ahead of almost anything else.
